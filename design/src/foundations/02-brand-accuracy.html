One lime, both themes

matchday declared two different limes — light oklch(0.94 0.19 124) → #d1ff61, dark oklch(0.9 0.21 123) → #c5f32d — while the transferred marketing pages hardcoded a third and a fourth (#b4ff39 ×126, #a3ff00 ×51). The split was not indecision. It happened because there was no token for lime as type, so the one fill value kept being bent to do two jobs it cannot do at once.

{{icon:x:13}} What went wrong
Four limes, two of them invented outside the token layer. Retinting the product meant a find-and-replace, so nobody did it.
{{icon:check:13}} What we ship
One fill (--brand, identical in both themes) plus one text token (--brand-text, which flips). Two jobs, two tokens.

Why #b4ff39 and not #dfff00

Near-black ink scores 15–17:1 on every lime in the running, so contrast does not pick the winner. Two things do: #b4ff39 is already proven in both themes across 126 usages in this repo, and standardising on it retires the largest block of hardcoded hex in one move. It is also marginally less glaring than #dfff00 on a white page.

One thing colour choice cannot fix. A bright lime fill is ~1.16:1 against a light page — and that is true of every candidate, including #dfff00 (1.09) and #a3ff00 (1.19). You cannot pick your way out of it; a lime light enough to be a lime is never 3:1 against white. Hence --brand-edge on light surfaces, and nothing in dark, where the fill already clears 16:1.

Also retired: #76B900, the marketing pages' light-mode lime text. It measures 2.31:1 — it was never passing. --brand-text at 4.62:1 replaces it.

Re-run the audit any time with node design/verify-contrast.mjs; it exits non-zero on a regression.
